# Writing Studio Memory Proposals — Angela's Review

**What this is:** Proposed additions to Artemis's Writing Studio memory — reusable guidance
that helps the AI write more like Amira's actual marketing team. Each card below is a candidate
rule or reference example. Please mark Approve, Reject, or Edit on each one. Approved items
become standing guidance that the AI applies when drafting any Amira content.

**How to use it:** Work through each section. A quick "A" or "R" in the checkbox is enough; use
the Edit line only when you want to change the wording before it goes in. You do not need to
approve everything — 15–20 strong rules beat 30 mediocre ones.

## Section 1 — Voice & Tone (7 proposals)

---

### V-1 — The mission line is structural, not decorative

**Proposal:** Every Amira document — regardless of format or audience — closes with the
mission tagline "Every Child Deserves the Chance to Become a Reader." Treat it as a required
structural element (footer or closing line), not flair. Never paraphrase or shorten it.

**Source:** Appears verbatim in footers/closing lines of every document sampled: all five
Product Overviews, all five Enablement guides, all six Research documents.

### V-2 — Lead with the design philosophy, not the feature list

**Proposal:** When introducing a product, open with what Amira is built to do (the mechanism
or design principle), not a list of features. Avoid "Amira does X, Y, and Z" opener structures.
Instead: "Amira was created for one purpose — to couple the Science of Reading with AI,
giving every child a pathway to the power of reading."

**Source:** NM Summer School Efficacy, Overview section, p. 2; Middle School Impact Study,
Overview section, p. 1. Identical language appears in both, confirming it is a standing pattern.

### V-3 — Confident declarative conclusions; no hedging on the headline

**Proposal:** When the evidence supports a clear conclusion, state it plainly and directly before
presenting the data. Reserve caveats for footnotes or a discrete "caveats" paragraph — do not
pre-hedge the main claim. Example pattern: "The evidence assembled in this paper supports a
clear conclusion. Amira is designed for equity, trained for equity, validated for equity, and
empirically demonstrates equity."

**Source:** Delivering Equity Whitepaper, Executive Summary, p. 2.

### V-4 — Parallel construction for multi-point summaries

**Proposal:** When listing multiple lines of evidence or product benefits, use strict grammatical
parallelism and a consistent noun-phrase or verb-phrase lead for each item. Avoid mixing
sentence types within the same list. Example: "Trained For Equity … Psychometrically
Equitable … Comparatively Equitable … Human Validated Equitable …"

**Source:** Delivering Equity Whitepaper, Five Lines of Evidence section, pp. 2–3.

### V-5 — Agentic, active verbs for product descriptions; never passive-feature language

**Proposal:** Describe what Amira does as an agent acting on behalf of students and teachers:
"Amira listens … analyzes … adapts … delivers … identifies … guides." Avoid passive constructions
("students are assessed by …") and static feature claims ("Amira has speech recognition").
The product is always doing something purposeful, in real time, for a specific person.

**Source:** Amira Assess one-pager ("Amira listens as students read aloud — capturing
authentic evidence of learning"); RFP/Student Experience doc throughout; Funding Guide
("Amira is an AI Learning Agent … turning data into action").

## Section 2 — Structure Patterns (6 proposals)

---

### S-1 — State-specific field guide skeleton

**Proposal:** State compliance field guides follow this five-part skeleton: (1) "About This Guide"
— one short paragraph naming the law, the audience, what the guide covers, and a mandatory
disclosure that Amira publishes it; (2) What the Law Requires — plain-language summary with
compliance timeline; (3) How to Evaluate Any Screener — vendor-neutral questions applied
equally to all tools; (4) Funding Options — with a standard disclaimer that allowability is
district-specific and must be confirmed with legal counsel; (5) Action Checklist — checkbox
items sized for a single team meeting.

**Source:** Illinois Field Guide, Maryland Field Guide, Michigan Field Guide — all three follow this
skeleton.

**Approve ☐ / Deny ☐ / Edit: ________________________**

---

### S-2 — Efficacy study skeleton

**Proposal:** Efficacy/research documents follow: (1) Key Findings callout box — three to four
bullets, each a standalone stat + so-what; (2) Overview — origin of Amira + the specific
research question; (3) Methodology — sample, measures, analytical strategy; (4) Results —
descriptive first, then inferential; (5) Conclusions — three to five named key achievements
using short bold headers; (6) "Learn how Amira can drive reading growth" CTA. Never open with
methodology; always lead with the finding.

**Source:** NM Summer School Efficacy; Middle School Impact Study — both follow this
structure.

**Approve ☐ / Deny ☐ / Edit: ________________________**

---

### S-3 — Funding/alignment guide: align → allow → assign

**Proposal:** Funding guides organize each federal program in three steps: (1) purpose of the
program in one sentence; (2) "How Amira Aligns" — bullet list of what Amira does that maps to
that purpose; (3) "Allowable Uses with Amira" — specific, concrete activities. Always close with
a funding disclaimer: allowable uses are district-specific and subject to audit; confirm with
legal counsel and finance team.

**Source:** Funding Guide (FG_05192026) for Title I, II, III, IV, IDEA sections.

**Approve ☐ / Deny ☐ / Edit: ________________________**

### S-4 — Whitepaper: hypothesis-test-refutation structure

**Proposal:** When rebutting a misconception or objection, use an explicit hypothesis-then-data
structure: state the specific prediction that would be true IF the concern were valid, then show
the data that tests it. Example: "If Amira were unable to listen accurately to ELLs, then Amira
would be giving those ELLs LOW scores … The data shows, in fact, that this year in New Mexico,
Amira is flagging 26% FEWER ELLs …" This pattern builds credibility and disarms objections
more cleanly than a defensive rebuttal.

**Source:** Delivering Equity Whitepaper, "The Evidence in Plain Terms" section, pp. 3–4.

**Approve ☐ / Deny ☐ / Edit: ________________________**

### S-5 — One-pager skeleton: hook stat → approach table → takeaways → CTA

**Proposal:** Evidence one-pagers (research briefs for sales use) open with the headline finding
as a visual stat or pull quote, then a compact three-row table (Sample & Setting / Measures /
Methodology), then three "Key Takeaways" bullets written for a district leader who has ten
seconds, then a QR code + URL CTA.

**Source:** Louisiana one-pager (Louisiana_RB_05192026); OP_Texas structure (same pattern).

**Approve ☐ / Deny ☐ / Edit: ________________________**

### S-6 — State-specific sales one-pager skeleton: bold headline claim → subpopulation proof → approvals/mandate alignment → CTA

**Proposal:** State-targeted one-pagers (for use in sales or field conversations in a specific
state) follow this four-part pattern: (1) an alliterative three-part headline claim using the
state's name and a high-salience outcome metric ("AI-Powered. Texas-Tested. Proven Results.");
(2) a "STAAR Results / [State Test] Results" section showing per-grade percentile-rank gains at
three dosage tiers (0–9 min / 10–19 min / 30+ min) as a bar chart; (3) a "Learning Acceleration
Across Every Subpopulation" section listing scale-score advantages for named subgroups (Black,
Hispanic, At-Risk, Economically Disadvantaged, Special Education, Emergent Bilingual); (4) an
approvals/mandate-alignment section listing specific state funding or program codes (TEA
approval numbers, HB references, TIA designations, TELPAS connections) with a URL CTA.
Always name the test by its state-specific acronym in the headline.

**Source:** OP_Texas one-pager (`OP_Texas_01262026.pdf`), all four sections.

## Section 3 — Audience Framing (6 proposals)

---

### A-1 — District leaders: lead with coherence and burden reduction, not accuracy stats

**Proposal:** When writing for district curriculum/assessment leaders, open with the problem
of fragmented systems and educator burden before introducing Amira. The promise is
coherence and reduced decision overhead, not raw accuracy numbers. Example: "Too often,
those funds are applied to disconnected programs that fragment instruction, increase educator
burden, and dilute impact."

**Source:** Funding Guide, opening paragraph; Instruct one-pager ("so teachers spend less
time diagnosing and more time teaching").

**Approve ☐ / Deny ☐ / Edit: ________________________**

---

### A-2 — State policy/compliance audiences: radical transparency about Amira's own stake

**Proposal:** In any document targeting a compliance or evaluation audience (state agency,
district procurement), include an explicit disclosure that Amira publishes the guide and has a
product relevant to the decision. Use this exact framing pattern: "A note on this guide: Amira
Learning publishes this guide, and Amira ISIP Assess is [one of three / one tool relevant to]
[state] requirements. We have made every effort to represent all tools accurately and fairly. The
authoritative source for [requirement] is [official source]."

**Source:** Michigan Field Guide, Illinois Field Guide, Maryland Field Guide — identical disclosure
block at the top of each.

### A-3 — English learner / equity audiences: lead with design intent, then empirical proof

**Proposal:** When writing for audiences concerned about equity for ELL or students of color,
structure the argument as design-intent first, then empirical validation. Never open with
statistics alone. Open with what Amira was built to do differently (e.g., "Amira does not
'recognize speech' or attempt to turn speech into text. This avoids the central challenge of
accent and dialect."), then present data as confirmation of that design intent.

**Source:** Delivering Equity Whitepaper, "Amira Is Designed for Equity" section, p. 4.

### A-4 — Teachers / field audiences: step-by-step, numbered, student's-eye-view

**Proposal:** When writing for teachers or field users (coaches, implementation leads), present
flows as numbered steps from the student's perspective, with each step's teacher implication
called out in a sub-bullet. Use present tense. Avoid abstract system descriptions. Example:
"STEP 3: Amira displays a 'Ready to Start?' prompt. The student clicks the green 'Let's do it!'
button. • This screen sets a positive, encouraging tone for the reading session."

**Source:** Amira Tutor Student Experience RFP response, Sections 1–2, throughout.

### A-5 — District leaders choosing Amira Tutor: the four-reason framework

**Proposal:** When writing for district administrators evaluating the Tutor product specifically,
organize the value proposition into exactly four named reasons, in this order: (1) in-the-moment,
Science of Reading–aligned coaching that scales; (2) students build confidence toward mastery
(including ELL, dyslexia, ADHD); (3) teachers have more insight and more time (Amira surfaces
skill gaps so teachers focus on what matters most, without adding instructional burden); (4)
built for MTSS and district strategy (Tier 1/2/3 framing, district-wide execution). This ordering
moves from the student experience → teacher benefit → district/systems payoff, and tracks how
a district leader thinks about adoption risk.

**Source:** Amira Tutor product overview (`Amira_Tutor_05192026.pdf`), "Why District Leaders
Choose Amira Tutor" section.

### A-6 — State-policy/legislative audiences: lead with the mandate, then the solution

**Proposal:** When writing for a state-specific compliance or policy audience, name the specific

Approved examples: "Amira is the only TEA-approved program that raises district proficiency

## Section 4 — Approved Terminology in Context (7 proposals)

### T-1 — "Learning Agent" is the master product identity term

**Proposal:** Amira's primary identity label is "AI Learning Agent" (or "learning agent"), not
"AI tutor," "platform," "tool," or "software." Use it as both the noun and the modifier. The full
formulation: "Amira is an AI Learning Agent grounded in the Science of Reading — designed to
continuously assess, instruct, and tutor students through a connected learning loop." Use
"learning agent" when one word is needed; "AI learning agent" when the full term fits.

**Source:** EGS one-pager headline; Funding Guide opening; Instruct one-pager. "Learning
agent" appears in all three folders.

**Proposal:** When writing about Amira's three-component system, always present the
components in this order — Assess, Instruct, Tutor — and frame them as a loop, not a list of
options. The canonical phrase: "Make your literacy work more coherent — by connecting
Assess → Instruct → Tutor into one evidence-based loop to drive student growth." The arrow

**Proposal:** "Science of Reading" is always capitalized (not "science of reading" or "science-of-
reading"). It appears in the first or second paragraph of virtually every document. It is not
introduced with a definition — it is used as a known standard the audience shares. Do not
explain it; invoke it.

### T-4 — "Dynamic Assessment" = the mechanism behind Assess, not a synonym for
"adaptive"

**Proposal:** Use "dynamic assessment" (lowercase or as a branded label "Dynamic Assessment")

Testing Reading. Start Observing It. Amira ISIP Assess is a dynamic reading assessment that
listens as students read aloud — capturing authentic evidence of learning." Do not use
"adaptive" as a synonym; adaptive describes instruction that changes based on results.
Dynamic describes the always-on, listening-while-reading assessment mechanism.

## Section 5 — Proof & Evidence Framing Language (5 proposals)

---

### E-1 — The standard evidence citation format for external studies

**Proposal:** When citing an independent study, use this format: [Author/organization] +
[year/date] + [scope: N students, districts, demographics] + [what was found]. Then add the
availability line. Example: "An independent study examined 79,084 students across 12
Louisiana school districts, 75% economically disadvantaged and 10% English learners. Students
using Amira showed statistically significant reading gains at every grade level from
kindergarten through grade 5. The study was conducted by Shah, Scanlan, and Wall for the
Instructure EdTech Collective in June 2025 and is available in full from Amira Learning."

**Source:** Illinois Field Guide, Part 2 (A Note on Amira ISIP Assess); Maryland Field Guide,
Part 3 (same passage). Cited identically in both.

### E-2 — Vendor-data disclaimer is mandatory when citing internal or outcome data

**Proposal:** Any claim sourced from Amira product documentation or internal outcome data
must carry a verification directive. Standard language: "All capability claims below are sourced
from Amira Learning product documentation. Confirm features and license inclusions directly
with your Amira rep at time of contracting." For outcome data from a single site: "These figures
reflect reported outcomes for a single district and school year. Contact [District] for current
data before citing in board or budget materials."

**Source:** Illinois Field Guide, "A Note on Amira ISIP Assess" header asterisk; Maryland Field
Guide, Part 3 asterisk and Baltimore County footnote.

### E-3 — Effect-size context: always anchor to a named external benchmark

**Proposal:** When reporting effect sizes, do not state them alone. Always anchor to a
recognized external benchmark. The two approved anchors are: John Hattie's "Zone of Desired
Effects" (effect sizes above 0.4) and "comparable to high-quality one-on-one tutoring." Do not
claim "large" or "significant" without the anchor. Example: "effect sizes of 0.35–0.40 —
comparable to high-quality one-on-one tutoring and well within the 'Zone of Desired Effects'
for educational interventions."

**Source:** Middle School Impact Study, Key Findings callout box, p. 1.

**Approve ☐ / Deny ☐ / Edit: ________________________**

---

### E-4 — Dosage framing: always tie the stat to the usage threshold

**Proposal:** When citing outcome data tied to usage, always state the usage threshold first,
then the outcome. Never lead with the outcome and bury the threshold. Standard pattern:
"Students [meeting/averaging] [X stories per week / X minutes per week] [outcome stat]
compared to [low-usage group description]." This prevents readers from attributing typical
results to every user.

**Source:** NM Summer School Efficacy, Results section ("students averaging 5+ stories per week
gained 4.68 more percentile points"); Middle School Impact Study, 23-24 Results ("students
who met the recommended weekly usage of 20 or more minutes … exhibited accelerated
growth").

**Approve ☐ / Deny ☐ / Edit: ________________________**

---

### E-5 — Subpopulation proof pattern: always name every subgroup, never aggregate them

**Proposal:** When presenting equity or subpopulation outcome data, list each named
subgroup individually with its own gain figure — never roll them into "all subpopulations" or
"diverse learners." The approved structure: one line per subgroup, with the scale-score or
percentile advantage stated as a number. The named subgroups in Amira materials are: Black,
Hispanic, All Other, At-Risk, Economically Disadvantaged, Special Education, Emergent
Bilingual. Presenting all seven (or however many apply) signals rigor and prevents cherry-
picking objections. Example anchor: "In a longitudinal study of 24,000+ students, Amira users
saw dramatic scale score advantages over non-users" — followed by the per-subgroup data.

**Source:** OP_Texas one-pager (`OP_Texas_01262026.pdf`), "Learning Acceleration Across
Every Subpopulation" section.
